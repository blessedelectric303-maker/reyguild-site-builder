# 10. Electrical Safety Procedures - Live Work, PPE and Lockout

> Work it dead, live-dead-live, gloves, PPE and boundaries. Read before your first job.
>
> Signature required

---


This is the company's written safety procedure for electrical work. It is a
summary written so it can be read on a phone in a driveway. It does not replace
the NEC (NFPA 70), NFPA 70E, or the OSHA standards - **those govern, and this
does not override them.** If this page and the code ever disagree, the code
wins and you stop and call the office.

## 1. The rule: work it dead

OSHA's electrical safe work practices require that live parts be **de-energized
before you work on or near them**. Energized work is the exception, not the
normal way we do a job, and it is allowed only where de-energizing introduces a
greater hazard or is genuinely infeasible - and then only with the right
protective equipment and the office informed first.

"It's only 120" is not a reason. Most electrocutions in this trade happen on
circuits everybody thought were harmless.

## 2. Lockout / tagout

Before you touch a conductor:

- Identify every source feeding the equipment, including anything backfed, any second panel, any generator or inverter, and any circuit that shares a neutral
- Open the disconnect
- **Apply your own lock and your own tag.** Your lock comes off by your hand and nobody else's
- Release stored energy - capacitors, motor loads
- Verify (see below) before touching anything

If you cannot lock it, you tag it AND you keep it under your direct visual
control. In an occupied home, assume somebody will flip a breaker back on the
moment you are out of the room, because they do.

## 3. Verifying dead: live, dead, live

**Every time. No exceptions. This is the step that saves your life.**

- Test your meter or tester on a **known live source**. Confirm it reads
- Test the conductors you are about to work on. Confirm they read zero, phase to phase, phase to neutral, and phase to ground
- Test your meter on a **known live source again**. Confirm it still reads

If step three fails, your meter died somewhere in the middle and step two told
you nothing. Start over with a different meter.

Use a rated meter (CAT III or CAT IV as appropriate for where you are testing).
A cheap non-contact pen is a hint, not a verification. Never use one alone to
call a circuit dead.

## 4. Rubber insulating gloves

If there is any possibility of contact with an energized conductor you wear
**rubber insulating gloves with leather protectors over them**. The leather is
not optional - it is what stops the rubber getting nicked, and a nicked glove
is not a glove.

- **Class 00** - rated to 500 volts maximum use
- **Class 0** - rated to 1000 volts maximum use

Residential and light commercial work is Class 00 or Class 0. Anything above
that is not our scope without a conversation first.

Care rules that are legal requirements, not suggestions:

- **Air-test and inspect your gloves before every single use.** Roll them, look for cuts, cracks, embedded material, swelling or any change in texture
- Gloves must be **electrically tested by a laboratory before first issue and every six months** while in service. Gloves that have not been tested inside that window cannot be used
- Keep them in the glove bag, out of sunlight, away from oil, solvents and sharp tools. Do not carry them loose in a bucket
- Any glove that fails inspection is taken out of service that minute and reported. You will never be charged for a failed glove

## 5. The rest of the PPE, every day, on the truck

You are expected to have all of this available on every job, not just when you
expect to need it:

- Safety glasses with side shields - worn any time you are cutting, drilling, pulling, or opening a panel
- **Arc-rated shirt and pants** when working on or near energized equipment, sleeves down and buttoned
- Arc-rated face shield with balaclava, or an arc flash hood, for work inside energized gear where an arc flash hazard exists
- Class E hard hat where there is head or overhead hazard
- Insulated (1000V rated) hand tools
- Fiberglass ladder. **Never an aluminum ladder near electrical work**
- Insulating blanket or barrier where an energized part cannot be avoided
- GFCI protection on all temporary power and cord-connected tools
- Boots in good condition, laced

**Remove all metal.** Watch, rings, chains, metal-framed glasses, belt buckle
where it can bridge. A wedding ring across a bus bar takes the finger.

## 6. Working inside a panel

- Know the available fault current and what you are standing in front of. If you do not know, you are not qualified for that panel today - call the office
- Stand to the side of the door, not square in front of it, and turn your face away as you open it
- One hand rule where you reasonably can. Keep the other hand out of the enclosure
- Never reach blind behind a panel or into a ceiling for a conductor you cannot see
- Do not defeat, bypass or jumper a safety interlock

## 7. Approach boundaries

Only a **qualified person** crosses the limited approach boundary toward an
exposed energized conductor, and only a qualified person wearing the correct
gloves and PPE crosses the restricted approach boundary.

An apprentice does not cross either boundary alone, ever. If you are an
apprentice and you are standing at a live panel by yourself, something has gone
wrong upstream of you - stop and call.

## 8. Ladders, holes and everything that is not electrical

Most of the people hurt in this trade are hurt by the ordinary things:

- Ladder on solid level footing, correct angle, tied off or footed, extended three feet past the roof line
- Never stand on the top cap or the top step
- Nothing carried up a ladder that stops you having a hand for it
- Fall protection where required by height
- Clear your own trip hazards as you go - cords, offcuts, wire spools
- Cut-resistant gloves for stripping and cutting. A knife toward yourself is a stitches call

## 9. When something goes wrong

- **Stop work.** You will never be disciplined for stopping a job over a legitimate safety concern. You may be disciplined for continuing anyway
- Report every injury the **same day it happens**, no matter how small. A small burn documented today is a claim; the same burn reported next month is a fight
- Report every near miss. A near miss is a free lesson and we would rather learn it from you than from an ambulance
- Report unsafe conditions **before** you proceed, not after
- If you are not trained or qualified for what is in front of you, say so. That is the correct answer and nothing bad happens to you for giving it
